When liquids break Newton's third law

Active systems have many unexpected and exciting properties which we do not see in equilibrium. One of them is that the interactions may not be symmetric: no longer is every action opposed by a reaction equal in magnitude, as Newton supposed. For example, if the particles making up a liquid catalyze a chemical reaction, this may mediate a non-reciprocal interaction.

Together with collaborators, I investigated what consequences this has close to the critical point of a liquid, where it undergoes a phase transition. We showed that such liquids exhibit exotic behaviours, which allow for finer control of the liquid droplets than would be possible in equilibrium: for example, the length-scales governing spontaneous fluctuations $C$ and the response to external stimuli $\chi$ can be independently controlled. This result may have implications for biological systems, which rely on the phase separation of active liquids to create order—many organelles inside the cell are liquid droplets where important biological functions are performed.
